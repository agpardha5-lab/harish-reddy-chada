Check the shipment before you pay the invoice

In wholesale, three documents tell the story of every supplier order: the purchase order you sent, the shipment that showed up at the dock, and the invoice that arrives asking to be paid. In a perfect world, all three say the same thing. In the real world, they often don't.

A case gets shorted. A substitute item shows up in place of the one you ordered. A price on the invoice doesn't match the price you agreed to. None of these are dramatic on their own, and most of them are honest mistakes. But if nobody compares the three documents, the business quietly pays for product it never received and carries stock numbers that were wrong from the moment the truck was unloaded.

Receiving is where accuracy starts

I think of receiving as the front door of inventory. Whatever gets counted there becomes the starting point for everything after it: what you can sell, what you need to reorder, and what you owe. If receiving is rushed and someone just signs for the pallet, every report downstream inherits that error.

Good receiving doesn't have to be slow. It means counting against the purchase order, noting what's short, damaged, or different, and recording it right away instead of relying on someone to remember later.

Match before you pay

Once receiving is recorded, the invoice check becomes simple. For each line, ask three questions:

  • Did we order it? The item and quantity should trace back to a purchase order.
  • Did we receive it? The quantity billed should match what was actually counted at the dock.
  • Is the price right? The unit cost should match what was agreed, including any deals or discounts.

When all three line up, the invoice is ready to pay. When they don't, it gets flagged and goes back to the supplier with specifics, not a vague "something looks off."

This is a perfect job for automation

Doing this by hand works when you have a few suppliers and a few orders a week. As volume grows, it turns into hours of comparing paper against screens. That's exactly the kind of repetitive, rule-based checking software should handle.

A well-built system can read the supplier invoice, pull up the matching purchase order and receiving record, compare them line by line, and only bring a person in when something doesn't match. AI tools make the first step much easier, because invoices come in every format imaginable, and reading them into structured data used to be the hardest part. People then spend their time on the exceptions instead of the routine.

It's also about the relationship

Catching mismatches isn't about distrusting suppliers. Clear records make conversations easier. When you can point to exactly which line was short and when it was received, issues get resolved quickly and without friction. Suppliers appreciate customers who are organized, and it builds trust in both directions.

That's one of the workflows I care about most in the ERP systems I'm building for distributors: receiving, purchasing, and invoices all tied to the same inventory records, so the check happens as part of the work instead of as an afterthought.
